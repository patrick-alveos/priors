### Flydubai co-pilot allegedly tried to crash Tel Aviv flight with an axe

**What happened.** Flydubai flight FZ1073 from Dubai to Tel Aviv plunged more than 17,000 feet in under two minutes last Wednesday after the co-pilot allegedly attacked the captain with a crash axe and tried to seize control. The UAE's prosecutor general has called it a terrorist attack, and Israeli PM Netanyahu said the co-pilot showed signs of 'Islamist radical indoctrination,' while reports note he had previously been barred from flying by Oman over extremist views.

**Potential implications.** The obvious question is whether this was ideological terrorism, a personal breakdown that happened to involve a plane, or some mix of both — and the answer matters a lot for how aviation regulators respond. If this turns into a confirmed radicalization case, expect tighter psychological vetting and background-sharing requirements for pilots across Gulf carriers, plus renewed scrutiny of how one country's no-fly flags (Oman's, in this case) get communicated to others. If it turns out closer to a mental-health crisis dressed in political language after the fact, the policy response should look more like pilot wellness screening than counterterrorism theater — but politically, the terrorism framing is likely to win regardless of what investigators eventually conclude, because it's more useful to more people. Either way, base rates for pilot-caused incidents remain extremely low (this is roughly the aviation equivalent of a black swan), so the proportionate response is probably narrower than the coverage volume suggests.

### G7 nations to release 100 million barrels of reserves to defuse Trump's diesel threat

**What happened.** UK diesel prices hit a record £2 a litre on 2 October, up 40.5% since late February, when the US-Israel war on Iran began disrupting Gulf crude and refined oil supplies. Donald Trump responded by threatening to ban US diesel exports to lower prices for American consumers ahead of November's elections, alarming European governments who warned the move would be 'catastrophic.' After emergency talks, G7 leaders agreed to release up to 100 million barrels of oil and diesel from their strategic reserves over the next four months to ease the squeeze and head off the export ban.

**Potential implications.** This is a reminder that energy security is still, eight months into the Iran disruption, a game of chicken between allies rather than a settled market. The G7's 100-million-barrel release is a stopgap — it buys time and signals coordination, but it doesn't fix the underlying supply gap from Gulf refining capacity knocked offline since February. If the release genuinely calms prices, expect Trump to quietly shelve the export ban threat (it was leverage, not necessarily policy he wanted to use); if diesel keeps climbing anyway, the ban becomes much more likely heading into election season, which would hit European refiners harder than anyone since the US has become a marginal diesel supplier during this crisis. Worth watching whether the four-month drawdown schedule holds — reserve releases announced with fanfare sometimes get quietly extended or front-loaded once markets test the sellers' resolve, as happened with SPR releases in prior crises. The deeper question is whether 100 million barrels (roughly a day and a half of global oil demand) is a credible deterrent or mostly theater; historically, reserve releases move prices modestly and temporarily unless paired with an actual resolution of the underlying supply shock, in this case the Iran war itself.

### Apple clamps down on full-disk access to keep AI agents from going rogue

**What happened.** Apple said it will add new limits to "full disk access" permissions on macOS, a control that currently lets apps read or modify almost any file on the system once granted. The company says the risk calculus has changed now that AI agents can act autonomously inside that access, and it wants to make sure the permission goes only to apps users genuinely intend to trust with it. The move follows reporting that Meta considered full-disk access adequate protection for an AI agent capable of reading user messages — a framing Apple apparently rejects.

**Potential implications.** The tension here is structural, not just a Meta-Apple spat: an AI agent that's actually useful on your computer needs to read your files, and an AI agent that can read your files is also one that can quietly exfiltrate or misuse them, with no human in the loop to notice. Apple tightening the gate is a bet that most users can't meaningfully consent to that tradeoff today, which effectively slows down what third-party agentic apps can do on the Mac relative to Apple's own first-party tools. That's also, not coincidentally, a competitive move — security justifications and platform control are hard to cleanly separate, which is the exact argument at the heart of antitrust scrutiny of Apple. Prediction markets put the odds of DOJ winning its antitrust case against Apple at 26%, up just 1 point this week — a sign that watchers don't see this particular policy change as materially shifting that case, though it's the kind of incident plaintiffs will likely cite as part of a broader pattern. The bigger open question is whether Apple's fix threatens to annoy power users enough that it pushes serious AI-agent workflows toward other platforms.

**The takes.**
