### Trump strikes Russian diesel deal, Zelensky calls it a gift to Putin

**What happened.** The Trump administration announced a deal allowing imports of Russian diesel, a move that runs against the broader Western push to cut off revenue to Moscow. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky condemned it as an "investment in war that must be ended, not prolonged." BBC's North America correspondent frames the deal as driven largely by domestic pressure to bring down fuel prices before next month's US midterm elections.

**Potential implications.** This is the kind of move that's easy to read as either cynical or merely pragmatic, and probably it's both. Sanctions regimes are leaky by design — carve-outs for energy, especially diesel which heats homes and runs trucks, have a long history of surviving even hawkish administrations because voters feel fuel prices directly and feel geopolitics abstractly. The timing a month before midterms is not subtle: if diesel prices tick down even slightly, that's a tangible win Republicans can point to, while the marginal effect on Russia's war financing is diffuse and hard for any voter to trace back to a ballot. The more interesting question is whether this becomes a template — if diesel carve-outs hold, does crude or refined product follow? Ukraine's allies in Europe, who've spent three years building sanctions architecture, will be watching for whether this is a one-off political fix or the start of erosion. Worth noting there's no market data here to anchor on, so this is mostly a bet on how much midterm-year political incentives bend energy policy versus how much the war itself constrains that bending.

## Business & Markets

### Burnham pledges to loosen non-compete clauses in UK job contracts

**What happened.** The UK prime minister, Andy Burnham, said restrictions preventing workers from joining rivals or starting competing businesses after leaving a job have "gone too far." He promised reforms to curb the use of non-compete clauses in employment contracts. No specific legislation, timeline, or enforcement mechanism was detailed in the announcement.

**Potential implications.** Non-competes are one of those policies that sound like they protect trade secrets but mostly just protect incumbents from competition — the US FTC tried to ban them outright in 2024 before courts blocked it, and the UK has been circling similar reform for years without acting. If Burnham's government actually follows through, the first-order effect is more worker mobility: engineers, salespeople, and executives can jump to competitors or start their own ventures without a year-long legal cooling-off period. The second-order effect is more interesting for founders and investors — looser non-competes tend to correlate with more startup formation and faster idea diffusion (this is part of the standard explanation for why Silicon Valley outcompeted Boston's Route 128 corridor, where non-competes were enforceable). The flip side is that firms investing heavily in training or proprietary R&D may respond by front-loading retention bonuses or tightening NDAs and non-solicitation clauses instead, so the net effect on talent hoarding could be smaller than headlines suggest. Watch for whether this becomes actual legislation or stays at the level of political rhetoric — UK governments have promised employment law reform before and delivered consultations instead of statutes.

### Ukrainian drones damage data center training Russia's top AI models

**What happened.** Ukraine struck a data center that houses supercomputers used to train AI models for Yandex, often described as "Russia's Google." The facility sustained damage to hardware specifically used for AI training workloads, according to reporting on the strike. It's part of a broader pattern of Ukrainian drone strikes targeting Russian infrastructure well behind the front lines.

**Potential implications.** Compute has quietly become a wartime target, which is a logical extension of a conflict that's already seen refineries, power plants, and now server farms hit. For AI watchers, the immediate effect is modest: one data center's worth of training capacity is a rounding error against the compute Russia can source domestically or through grey-market channels, so this won't meaningfully slow Yandex's AI roadmap on its own. The more interesting question is precedent-setting: if AI infrastructure is now explicit military target, that changes the risk calculus for anyone building large training clusters in or near conflict zones, and raises the question of how data center operators elsewhere (Taiwan's chip fabs come to mind as the obvious analogy) think about physical security versus cyber risk. Worth watching whether this becomes a pattern or stays a one-off; a single strike tells you little about whether compute infrastructure is now a standing target category in this war.

### A Stranger Saw His Plea on the News and Showed Up With a Tarp
