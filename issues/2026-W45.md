### Paraguay's president impeached and jailed at home after a bank he favored collapsed

**What happened.** Santiago Peña, Paraguay's rightwing president, resigned and was then impeached by Congress after Ueno Bank, which had grown from an obscure digital lender to one claiming roughly 60% of the country's adult customers under his administration, was suspended over irregularities. The collapse left more than half of Paraguay's population unable to access their bank accounts, triggering protests. Minutes after being impeached, Peña was placed under house arrest and is now under investigation for allegedly using political influence to favor the bank's rapid expansion.

**Potential implications.** This is the kind of story that looks, in retrospect, like an obvious bubble: a digital bank goes from nothing to 60% market penetration in a few years under a friendly government, and nobody asks too hard how. The pattern — rapid, politically-blessed expansion followed by a sudden freeze — rhymes with plenty of emerging-market banking crises, and the base rate for 'fast-growing bank with cozy government ties turns out fine' is not great. The immediate question for Paraguay is whether this stays a domestic political and liquidity crisis or spreads into a broader confidence run on the rest of the banking system; with 60% of the population locked out of their money, the pressure for a quick, credible resolution (deposit guarantees, central bank backstop, or both) is intense, and botching that risks turning a bank failure into a currency and investment crisis. For the region, expect increased scrutiny of fintech-style 'bank' expansions elsewhere that have grown unusually fast with unusually friendly regulators — this is a useful cautionary tale even without a direct causal link. The political angle is almost as interesting as the financial one: a sitting president impeached and put under house arrest within the same week is a fast institutional response by regional standards, which could reflect either unusually functional checks and balances or an elite consensus that Peña had become a liability worth cutting loose quickly to protect the system's credibility.

For travelers, the base rate of hoax threats actually containing a bomb is close to zero historically, so the practical risk to any individual flight stays low even as the aggregate disruption (delays, rebookings, media disruption) rises. ### A diet-heart study finds a subtle signal, and plenty of reasons to shrug

**What happened.** Researchers analyzed data from 7,256 adults in the Maastricht Study, a Dutch population cohort, looking for links between diet quality and heart rate variability (HRV), a marker of autonomic nervous system function. People who more closely followed the DASH diet or the Dutch Healthy Diet pattern showed higher very-low-frequency (VLF) HRV power, an effect concentrated among those not taking beta-blockers. After full statistical adjustment, the more commonly cited time-domain HRV measures showed no significant association with diet at all.

**Potential implications.** This is a cross-sectional snapshot, not a trial, so the honest reading is correlation with a side order of maybe-causation. VLF HRV itself is a somewhat obscure and contested metric — researchers still argue about what physiological process it actually tracks, which makes it a shakier foundation for health claims than, say, LDL cholesterol or blood pressure. The fact that the more standard time-domain HRV measures showed nothing after adjustment is the kind of result that should make you discount the headline finding rather than inflate it; in observational nutrition science, a pattern that only shows up in one of several measured outcomes is more often noise than signal, especially once you account for how many comparisons got run to find it. None of this means DASH or Dutch-style eating patterns are bad for you — the existing evidence on DASH and cardiovascular risk is reasonably solid from other angles — but this particular study adds a thin, hedged data point rather than a load-bearing one. The beta-blocker interaction is an interesting thread (drugs that blunt autonomic signaling would plausibly mask diet-driven HRV effects), but it's also exactly the kind of subgroup finding that looks compelling until someone tries to replicate it.
