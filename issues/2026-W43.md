### Ebola reaches a fourth African country as Kenya reports its second death

**What happened.** An outbreak that began in the Democratic Republic of Congo in mid-May has now spread to Uganda, Kenya, and Nigeria. In Kenya, a 65-year-old woman died after contracting the virus from a male relative who had traveled from DRC, making her the outbreak's second Kenyan fatality. Nigeria confirmed its first Ebola case in over a decade: a Ugandan-born woman, wife of a Swiss diplomat, who fell ill after traveling from Uganda and was diagnosed in Abuja; Swiss officials say she had not been to DRC and likely caught the virus in Uganda, though the exact source remains unknown.

**Potential implications.** The geography here is the worrying part, not the case count. Four countries in five months, with the latest transmission chain unexplained, suggests this outbreak has more routes of spread than contact tracers have mapped. That's the difference between a contained flare-up and something that needs sustained international attention for months. Historically, Ebola outbreaks in well-resourced health systems get stamped out within a localized cluster — DRC, Uganda, and Kenya all have institutional experience with this virus by now, which is the main reason this hasn't become 2014-level West Africa. Nigeria is the wild card: it's the continent's most populous country, with Lagos alone housing over 20 million people in dense conditions, and its last Ebola scare (2014) was handled well but under very different circumstances. The unresolved question — how did a woman with no DRC travel history catch it in Uganda — matters more than any single death toll, because an unmapped transmission source means more unmapped cases are plausible. Watch for whether health ministries can trace her contacts in Uganda before symptoms show up somewhere unexpected; if they can't, expect the case count to keep ticking upward through November.

**The takes.**
- [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6x2355448eyo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) frames the Kenyan deaths as a tight, traceable family cluster, emphasizing that the second victim's infection came directly from caring for the first known case rather than from any new, unexplained exposure.
