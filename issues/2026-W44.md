**Potential implications.** This is the sharpest flashpoint yet in a fight over Polish judicial legitimacy that's been simmering since 2015, when the then-ruling PiS party packed the tribunal with loyalists the current Tusk government has never recognized as properly appointed. Riot police at a constitutional court is the kind of image that normally shows up in countries sliding toward serious institutional breakdown, not EU member states — but Poland has spent a decade in a weird limbo where the rule-of-law crisis is real yet contained, mostly fought through dueling legal rulings and Brussels infighting rather than street confrontation. The open question is whether this is the moment it stops being contained: does Nawrocki, who has limited formal power over the courts but plenty of symbolic leverage, escalate further, or does this fizzle into another unresolved stalemate like the last several? Watch for whether this accelerates calls to tap frozen Russian assets or restructure production contracts to compress the timeline, since the money being approved matters less than whether anyone can actually build the missiles faster.

A convoy brought in supplies and evacuated hundreds of civilians, including children, from among the few thousand people still living there. Despite warnings that remaining residents faced starvation, hundreds reportedly chose to stay in the town rather than leave.

**Potential implications.** One town's ceasefire is not a ceasefire; localized truces for humanitarian access have happened periodically throughout this war without translating into broader de-escalation, so the base rate for this becoming a template rather than a one-off is low. What's more interesting is what it reveals: if conditions were bad enough that both sides agreed to pause fighting to prevent starvation, the siege dynamics in occupied territory may be worse than has been visible from outside. The fact that hundreds stayed despite the dangers is its own data point — probably some mix of elderly residents unable to travel, people unwilling to abandon property under occupation, and those wary of what evacuation to Ukrainian-controlled territory might mean for them long-term. Watch whether this operation gets repeated; a second humanitarian window would suggest an emerging, if narrow, channel of coordination rather than a singular exception.

### Binance loses $6.3bn in what may be the largest theft in history, North Korea the prime suspect

**What happened.** Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange, disclosed a $6.3bn hack on Thursday that analysts believe is the biggest crypto heist — and possibly the biggest theft of any kind — on record. Security firms including TRM Labs have attributed the attack to TraderTraitor, a North Korean hacking unit also known as the Lazarus Group. Binance says it will cover all customer losses and has so far managed to freeze roughly $3bn of the stolen assets as it works to trace the rest.

**Potential implications.** The headline number is eye-catching but the more interesting fact is the base rate: North Korea has been doing this for years, and crypto theft reportedly accounts for roughly half of its foreign currency income. That means $6.3bn isn't just a one-off embarrassment for Binance — it's a meaningful chunk of financing for a sanctioned state's weapons programs, which should worry policymakers more than crypto holders. For the industry, Binance covering losses out of pocket is the kind of move that only a dominant, well-capitalized exchange can make; a smaller platform hit this hard would likely be insolvent by Monday. Expect renewed regulatory pressure on exchange custody practices and on-chain forensics firms, though enforcement against North Korean state hackers has historically been more symbolic than effective — freezing $3bn is good, but $3.3bn reportedly got away, and recovery rates on past Lazarus Group hacks have been uneven at best.

### OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, and Anthropic agree on one opt-out button for AI scraping

**What happened.** The Verge reports that OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, and Anthropic will adopt a shared technical standard, a "policy token," that lets publishers set their AI-training preferences once and have it apply across all participating companies. Previously, publishers had to configure scraping permissions separately for each AI company, often through inconsistent robots.txt rules or individual negotiations. The standard was first reported by Search Engine Roundtable before The Verge's coverage.

**Potential implications.** This is less a truce than an attempt to standardize the terms of an ongoing extraction. A single opt-out token lowers the transaction cost for publishers to say no, which sounds like a win, but it also lets the AI companies claim good-faith compliance without changing the underlying economics: most publishers still have little leverage to negotiate payment rather than just permission. Watch whether adoption becomes genuinely industry-wide (smaller AI labs and scrapers have no obligation to honor the token) or whether it mainly serves as a liability shield for the big four while enforcement remains voluntary. The more interesting question is whether this standard quietly becomes the de facto legal baseline in future copyright litigation, since courts have been looking for exactly this kind of "reasonable technical measure" to assess good-faith behavior on both sides.
