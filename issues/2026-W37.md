### Houthis seize Yemen's Red Sea coast and a key strait island, raising fresh shipping and oil risks

**What happened.** Yemen's Iran-backed Houthi movement has rapidly seized control of the country's entire Red Sea coastline, capturing the port city of Mocha and, most recently, the island of Perim inside the Bab al-Mandab strait — one of the world's busiest oil and shipping chokepoints. Forces loyal to the UN-backed government in Aden withdrew from the area in what reporters describe as one of the Houthis' most significant military gains in years. Aid workers say nearly 50,000 civilians have fled the fighting, with villages emptied and displaced families facing dwindling food supplies.

**Potential implications.** Bab al-Mandab carries something like 10% of global seaborne oil trade and a large share of container traffic between Asia and Europe, so a hostile force controlling both shores of its narrowest point is the kind of thing that shows up in tanker insurance premiums within days, not months. The Houthis already spent 2023-24 disrupting Red Sea shipping with missiles and drones from a much weaker territorial position; holding Perim and the coast gives them more reach with less effort, and shippers who'd cautiously started returning to the route may reroute around the Cape of Good Hope again, adding roughly a week and meaningful cost to Asia-Europe voyages. The deeper question is whether this is a local power grab exploiting a government in disarray, or a coordinated move tied to the broader Iran-US confrontation — the timing next to that wider war is suspicious but not proof of coordination. Either way, expect Saudi Arabia (whose own oil exports transit this strait) and Egypt (worried about Suez traffic) to lobby hard for US or international naval action, while the humanitarian toll — tens of thousands displaced in the world's poorest country — gets treated as a footnote to the strategic story, which is its own kind of implication worth sitting with.

### Ahead of October budget, UK weighs squeezing banks harder — while Jamie Dimon quietly pushes back

**What happened.** With chancellor John Healey's October budget approaching, the TUC has asked Andy Burnham to reverse a bank surcharge cut, estimating this would raise £9bn over four years. Separately, a Guardian letter from economist Gerald Holtham argues a windfall tax isn't even necessary — the Bank of England already hands commercial banks roughly £24bn a year by paying its 3.75% policy rate on about £640bn of their reserves. Meanwhile, JPMorgan chief Jamie Dimon met Burnham and Healey to warn that raising bank taxes could put UK investment and jobs at risk.

**Potential implications.** This is the classic pre-budget dance: unions and economists lay out ways to extract more from a politically unpopular but economically powerful sector, while that sector's most prominent representative shows up in person to remind everyone what leverage looks like. The interesting wrinkle here is that there are two entirely different proposals in play — a surcharge hike (a straightforward tax increase) versus ending reserve interest payments (which isn't a tax at all, just the government declining to subsidize banks via monetary operations). The latter is analytically cleaner: it doesn't touch bank profits directly, it just stops what is effectively a fiscal transfer dressed up as monetary policy, and several other central banks have already tiered or reduced reserve remuneration for exactly this reason. Whether Healey picks the surcharge route, the reserves route, both, or neither will say a lot about how much weight a Dimon visit actually carries versus how much political cover the TUC's £9bn figure provides. Base rate for these things: governments facing a fiscal gap and a photogenic villain (profitable banks, in this case) usually do something, but rarely the maximum ask — expect a partial surcharge restoration rather than a full assault on reserve interest, since the latter risks accusations of central bank interference.

**The takes.**

### A five-minute sniff of coffee nudged mood and brain waves in a very small study

**What happened.** Researchers exposed 20 healthy young adults to coffee aroma for five minutes and tracked their mood and brain activity via EEG before and after. They found reductions in several negative mood measures and an uptick in an EEG-derived relaxation index, though heart rate and heart-rate variability didn't budge. The study had no control group, so it can't actually show the aroma caused these changes rather than, say, the passage of time or participants' expectations about coffee.

**Potential implications.** Twenty people and no control condition is about as far from a randomized trial as you can get while still publishing a paper, so the honest update here is small: maybe coffee smell does something to mood, maybe it's expectation effects from a lifetime of associating that smell with a caffeine hit and a cozy ritual, and the study design can't tell these apart. The EEG relaxation signal is more interesting than the self-reported mood shifts, since it's harder to consciously fake, but a single uncontrolled EEG readout in 20 subjects is thin evidence for anything beyond 'worth a bigger study.' If this replicates with a proper control odor and a larger sample, it'd feed into a growing (if scientifically shaky) wellness-industry interest in ambient scent as a cheap mood lever — think coffee-scented candles marketed for calm rather than caffeine. If it doesn't replicate, it joins the large pile of small psychology findings that quietly vanish on the second look; base rates for that outcome in underpowered single studies are not great.
