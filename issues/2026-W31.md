### Your DNA has receipts from at least two more extinct human relatives we've never seen

**What happened.** A new genetic study led by UC Berkeley researchers found signatures of interbreeding with two previously unknown archaic hominin lineages, on top of the known contributions from Neanderthals and Denisovans. One group, dubbed a 'ghost lineage,' mixed with ancestors of modern Africans more than 50,000 years ago and left no known descendants of its own. The other apparently diverged from other hominins in Eurasia over 1.7 million years ago, far earlier than any previously documented human-hybridization event.

**Potential implications.** There's no fossil record for either group, so everything we know about them is inferred purely from statistical patterns in living people's genomes — which is a bit like reconstructing a car crash from skid marks with no wreckage. That's not crazy science (this is basically how Denisovans were first identified, from a finger bone plus genomics), but it means the confidence interval on 'what these things actually were' is wide. The bigger update is about the shape of human origins generally: the old story of a single lineage progressing linearly to modern humans keeps getting replaced by something messier — multiple contemporaneous hominin populations mixing, diverging, and going extinct across hundreds of thousands of years, with Africa itself hosting more diversity than assumed. If this pattern holds, expect more 'ghost lineages' to surface as sequencing of diverse African populations improves, since most ancient-DNA work to date has been Eurasia-heavy for preservation reasons having nothing to do with where interesting stuff happened.

### An AI model tries to spot suicide risk from routine ER triage data, no questions asked

**What happened.** Researchers built and tested five machine-learning models — logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and a neural network — to predict whether an emergency department patient will engage in suicide-related behavior within 30 days. The models used only structured data collected at triage, things like vitals, demographics, and chief complaint, rather than any disclosure-based screening questionnaire. Performance was measured across several metrics, including AUROC, AUPRC, recall, and F2 score, comparing how each algorithm balances catching true risk cases against false alarms.

**Potential implications.** The pitch here is obvious: suicide screening in EDs is inconsistent partly because it depends on patients volunteering information, and busy triage nurses don't always have time to ask. A model that flags risk passively from data already being collected could, in theory, catch people who'd otherwise slip through. But the base rate problem looms large — suicide-related behavior within 30 days of an ED visit is rare even among high-risk populations, so even a model with a strong AUROC can produce a lot of false positives in absolute terms, which matters a great deal when a false positive might mean an involuntary hold or a disruptive intervention. There's also the harder question of what happens after the flag: an alert without a matching clinical workflow (extra staff time, a warm handoff to psychiatry, actual capacity to act) risks becoming either ignored noise or, worse, a liability shield that substitutes for real assessment. This is early-stage validation work, not a deployed tool, so the real test — does it change outcomes in a live ED, and does it work equitably across demographic groups — hasn't happened yet. I'd want to see prospective trials and subgroup performance breakdowns before treating this as more than a promising research direction.
