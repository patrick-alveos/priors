Separately, a federal court ruled that the administration's blanket hold on wind project permits violates the law, striking down the broader policy the payouts were meant to complement. The two developments point in opposite directions: one buys out wind developers with cash, the other says the government can't simply freeze the industry by fiat.

**Potential implications.** The interesting thing here isn't that the administration opposes wind — that's been clear since day one — it's the mismatch between tactics and legal reality. Individually negotiated buyouts like the RWE deal are expensive but legally clean: nobody can sue you for paying them to stop. So expect more of the former and less of the latter going forward, which actually raises the government's total cost of slowing wind deployment rather than lowering it. For developers, this is a mixed signal: courts are a real backstop against wholesale bans, but the administration clearly has both the will and the budget to buy out specific projects it dislikes, and $1.2bn suggests the number isn't a constraint. Worth watching whether other developers now treat a buyout offer as a decent outside option rather than fighting permitting battles for years — RWE's price could become the going rate.

### Trump signs new executive orders narrowing birthright citizenship, again

**What happened.** On August 6, President Trump signed a fresh set of executive orders that ban so-called 'birth tourism' and widen the categories of parents whose children would not automatically receive US citizenship at birth. This follows an earlier attempt from his administration to redefine birthright citizenship that was blocked in the courts. The new orders target visitors who travel to the US specifically to give birth, as well as additional groups previously covered by the 14th Amendment's citizenship guarantee.

**Potential implications.** Birthright citizenship in the US rests on the 14th Amendment's text and an 1898 Supreme Court precedent (Wong Kim Ark) holding that almost anyone born on US soil is a citizen 'subject to the jurisdiction thereof.' An executive order cannot rewrite a constitutional amendment or overturn Supreme Court precedent by itself, so the realistic path here is litigation: expect swift lawsuits, likely a nationwide injunction within weeks, and eventually a Supreme Court test of whether any exceptions can be carved out administratively. The base rate for presidents winning novel constitutional reinterpretations via unilateral executive action is low. The more interesting question is how much birth tourism actually matters in scale versus symbolism — if it's a small phenomenon, the order's practical effect on immigration numbers is modest, but its effect on litigation activity, state-level ID/passport confusion for affected families, and the broader signal of intent to keep testing this boundary is real regardless of the legal outcome.

**The takes.**
- [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cn9nz5qwl8jo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss) notes that birth tourism, while politically potent as a talking point, remains a relatively small and hard-to-measure share of US births, raising the question of whether the policy is aimed more at signaling than at numbers.

### A fingertip patch could track Parkinson's drug levels through sweat

**What happened.** Researchers have developed a battery-free, wearable sensor that attaches to a fingertip and monitors levodopa, the primary medication used to treat Parkinson's disease, by analyzing sweat. The patch is designed to give faster readings than current methods, which typically rely on blood draws or self-reported symptoms. The goal is to help clinicians personalize dosing schedules based on how an individual's body actually processes the drug over time.

**Potential implications.** Levodopa dosing is famously finicky — too little and symptoms return, too much and patients get dyskinesia, and the therapeutic window narrows as the disease progresses. A cheap, continuous, battery-free way to measure drug levels in real time could let doctors adjust dosing before problems show up rather than after, which is the difference between reactive and proactive care. The bigger story here isn't really Parkinson's specifically — it's the broader trend of wearable biosensors moving from tracking vague wellness metrics (steps, heart rate) toward tracking actual pharmacokinetics, which is a much higher bar of clinical usefulness. If this works and gets validated in larger trials, similar sweat-based patches could plausibly extend to other drugs with narrow therapeutic windows (lithium, certain anticoagulants, chemotherapy agents). The honest caveat: early-stage sensor papers have a poor track record of reaching the clinic, and sweat-based measurement has historically struggled with reliability across different skin types, hydration levels, and activity. This is a promising proof of concept, not a change to how Parkinson's is treated today.
